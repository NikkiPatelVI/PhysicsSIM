5 Big Ideas for Unit 3

1
Work requires displacement in the direction of force. A force perpendicular to motion (like normal force on level ground, or centripetal force in circular motion) does zero work — no matter how large it is.
2
W_net = ΔKE — the work-energy theorem is everything. You don't need kinematics when you have energies. If you know KE_i and all the work done, you know KE_f instantly.
3
Conservation of energy is the fastest path in frictionless problems. Set KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f and solve. No need to find time, acceleration, or forces separately.
4
Friction converts mechanical energy into thermal energy — it doesn't destroy energy. ΔE_thermal = f_k · d. Total energy (mechanical + thermal) is always conserved; mechanical energy is not.
5
Power = rate of energy transfer. Two motors doing the same work are not equivalent if one takes twice as long. P = W/t and P = Fv — both show up on the exam.

6 Common Traps

TRAP
"The block moves 5 m, so friction does 5 J of work." Wrong — you need f_k, not just d. Also check: is W_friction = −f_k · d (negative), not +f_k · d.
TRAP
Setting h = 0 at the wrong place. You can set the reference anywhere, but you must be consistent within the same problem. PE is relative — only ΔPE matters.
TRAP
Using conservation of energy when friction is present. The simple KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f form only works with no friction. Add W_nc to the left side: KE_i + PE_i + W_nc = KE_f + PE_f.
TRAP
Confusing mass doubling effects on KE vs PE. KE = ½mv² — doubling mass doubles KE (for same v). PE = mgh — doubling mass doubles PE. But doubling v quadruples KE. These are different.
TRAP
Thinking normal force does work on an inclined block. Normal force is always perpendicular to displacement along the slope → W_N = 0. Only gravity and friction components do work along the slope.
TRAP
Forgetting PE_s = ½kx², not kx. Spring PE is quadratic in displacement, not linear. Hooke's law F = kx is linear; the energy stored is the area under that force-displacement graph = ½kx².

6 Must-Know Scenarios

Scenario 1 — Frictionless Ramp

A ball of mass m starts at height h from rest and slides down a frictionless ramp.

At the bottom: All PE_g has converted to KE. Set h_ref = bottom.

mgh = ½mv² → v = √(2gh)

Mass cancels — every object reaches the same speed regardless of mass. Speed depends only on height dropped.

Scenario 2 — Ramp with Friction

Same ramp but with kinetic friction coefficient μ_k over horizontal distance d.

Use the extended conservation equation:

mgh − f_k · d = ½mv²  where f_k = μ_k · mg cosθ

The friction term subtracts from the available energy. More friction or more distance → less final KE.

Scenario 3 — Spring Launch

A spring (constant k) compressed by x launches a block of mass m on a frictionless surface.

All spring PE converts to KE:

½kx² = ½mv² → v = x√(k/m)

Doubling compression x doubles the launch speed. Doubling mass reduces speed by √2.

Scenario 4 — Block Sliding to a Stop

A block with initial speed v slides on a surface with friction coefficient μ_k until it stops.

All KE lost to friction:

½mv² = f_k · d = μ_k mg · d → d = v²/(2μ_k g)

Stopping distance goes as v² — doubling initial speed quadruples stopping distance.

Scenario 5 — Vertical Spring-Mass

A block dropped onto a vertical spring. At maximum compression x, the block has momentarily stopped.

From release height h to max compression (set h_ref at compressed position):

mg(h + x) = ½kx²

The block falls an extra distance x into the spring, so total height drop is (h + x).

Scenario 6 — Power Up a Ramp

A motor lifts mass m a vertical height h in time t at constant speed.

Constant speed means no ΔKE — all work goes into PE_g:

P = W/t = mgh/t  (or P = Fv = mgv)

At constant speed the motor force equals mg exactly. P = mgv is the cleaner form when speed is given.

Practice Problems

1. A 2 kg block slides from rest down a frictionless ramp of height 3.2 m. What is its speed at the bottom?

Use conservation of energy (no friction):

mgh = ½mv² → v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.8 × 3.2) = √(62.72) ≈ 7.9 m/s

Mass cancels — the 2 kg is irrelevant here.

2. A 5 kg block moving at 6 m/s slides 4 m across a surface with μ_k = 0.30 before stopping. How much work did friction do?

W_friction = −f_k · d = −μ_k mg · d

W_f = −(0.30)(5)(9.8)(4) = −58.8 J

Check with work-energy theorem: ΔKE = 0 − ½(5)(6²) = −90 J. The 58.8 J removed by friction is less than 90 J — the block doesn't stop in 4 m. Let's find the actual stopping distance:

d = v²/(2μ_k g) = 36/(2 × 0.30 × 9.8) = 36/5.88 ≈ 6.1 m

3. A spring with k = 800 N/m is compressed 0.15 m and launches a 0.5 kg ball on a frictionless track. What is the ball's speed when it leaves the spring?

PE_s = KE: ½kx² = ½mv²

v = x√(k/m) = 0.15 × √(800/0.5) = 0.15 × √1600 = 0.15 × 40 = 6.0 m/s

4. A 70 kg person runs up a 4 m staircase in 3 s. What is their average power output?

P = W/t = mgh/t

P = (70)(9.8)(4)/3 = 2744/3 ≈ 915 W (about 1.2 horsepower)

5. A 3 kg block starts at rest at height 5 m on a ramp (θ = 30°) with μ_k = 0.20. What is its speed at the bottom?

Find the ramp length: L = h/sinθ = 5/sin30° = 5/0.5 = 10 m

Friction force along ramp: f_k = μ_k · mg cosθ = 0.20 × 3 × 9.8 × cos30° = 0.20 × 3 × 9.8 × 0.866 ≈ 5.09 N

Work by friction: W_f = −5.09 × 10 = −50.9 J

Energy equation: mgh + W_f = ½mv²

(3)(9.8)(5) − 50.9 = ½(3)v² → 147 − 50.9 = 1.5v² → v² = 64.1 → v ≈ 8.0 m/s

Without friction: v = √(2×9.8×5) = 9.9 m/s — friction cost about 1.9 m/s.

Annotated FRQ — Spring Launch + Ramp

A 0.4 kg block is held against a horizontal spring (k = 500 N/m) compressed 0.20 m on a frictionless surface. The spring is released and the block travels along the surface and then up a ramp (height 1.5 m) with μ_k = 0.25 over a ramp length of 3.0 m. (g = 9.8 m/s²)

(a) What is the block's speed immediately after leaving the spring?

Show answer

½kx² = ½mv² → v = x√(k/m) = 0.20 × √(500/0.4) = 0.20 × √1250 = 0.20 × 35.36 ≈ 7.07 m/s

1 pt — correct formula 1 pt — correct answer with units

(b) How much work does friction do on the block as it travels up the 3.0 m ramp?

Show answer

Normal force on ramp: N = mg cosθ, where sinθ = h/L = 1.5/3.0 = 0.5 → θ = 30°

N = 0.4 × 9.8 × cos30° = 0.4 × 9.8 × 0.866 ≈ 3.39 N

W_f = −μ_k · N · L = −0.25 × 3.39 × 3.0 ≈ −2.54 J

1 pt — correct normal force on slope 1 pt — correct sign and magnitude

(c) What is the block's speed at the top of the ramp?

Show answer

KE_i + W_f = KE_f + ΔPE_g

½mv₀² + W_f = ½mv_f² + mgh

½(0.4)(7.07²) + (−2.54) = ½(0.4)v_f² + (0.4)(9.8)(1.5)

9.99 − 2.54 = 0.2v_f² + 5.88

0.2v_f² = 9.99 − 2.54 − 5.88 = 1.57

v_f = √(1.57/0.2) = √7.85 ≈ 2.80 m/s

1 pt — correct energy equation with friction 1 pt — correct speed

(d) Compare the block's final KE at the top to its initial spring PE. Explain the difference using energy conservation.

Show answer

Initial PE_s = ½kx² = ½(500)(0.20²) = 10.0 J

Final KE = ½(0.4)(2.80²) ≈ 1.57 J

The remaining 8.43 J was transferred to: gravitational PE_g = mgh = 5.88 J, and thermal energy via friction = 2.54 J.

Total: 1.57 + 5.88 + 2.54 = 9.99 ≈ 10.0 J ✓ (total energy conserved)

1 pt — identifies PE_g and E_thermal as recipients 1 pt — shows total energy is conserved

5-Point Strategy for Energy Problems

1
Identify initial and final states. Label what you know at each: speed, height, spring compression.
2
Choose your reference height (h = 0). The bottom of the path is usually cleanest — it zeroes out the final PE_g.
3
Check for friction. If yes, compute W_nc = −f_k · d and use the extended equation. If no friction, use the simple KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f.
4
Write one equation. Plug in all known terms on both sides, then solve for the unknown.
5
Sanity check. With friction, final KE + PE should be less than initial. Without friction, it should be equal. Energy is never created.

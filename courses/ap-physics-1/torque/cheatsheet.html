Angular Kinematics

Angular Displacement
θ = s / r
Angle swept in radians, defined as arc length divided by radius. 2π rad = 360°, so one full revolution = 2π rad.
θ — angular displacement (rad) s — arc length (m) r — radius (m)
Where does this come from?

A radian is defined so that an arc of length equal to the radius subtends exactly 1 rad. That means for any circle, θ (in rad) = s/r by definition. The conversion 2π rad = 360° follows from the full circumference being s = 2πr, giving θ = 2πr/r = 2π. Always convert degrees to radians before plugging into kinematics equations.

Angular Velocity
ω = Δθ / Δt
Rate of change of angular displacement, in rad/s. Direction is given by the right-hand rule: curl fingers in the direction of rotation, thumb points along ω.
ω — angular velocity (rad/s) Δθ — change in angular displacement (rad) Δt — time interval (s)
Where does this come from?

Exactly analogous to linear velocity v = Δx/Δt — replace displacement x with angle θ. Angular velocity is a vector whose magnitude is the spin rate and whose direction (by right-hand rule) tells you which way the axis points. For AP 1, you mostly need the magnitude; direction matters mainly when angular momentum vectors are added or compared.

Angular Acceleration
α = Δω / Δt
Rate of change of angular velocity, in rad/s². Positive α means speeding up in the positive-θ direction (or slowing down in the negative-θ direction).
α — angular acceleration (rad/s²) Δω — change in angular velocity (rad/s) Δt — time interval (s)
Where does this come from?

Direct analog of linear acceleration a = Δv/Δt, with ω replacing v. Net torque causes angular acceleration the same way net force causes linear acceleration — the rotational version of Newton's second law is Στ = Iα. Sign convention: pick a positive rotation direction (usually counterclockwise) and keep it consistent throughout the problem.

Rotational Kinematics
ω = ω₀ + αt  |  θ = ω₀t + ½αt²  |  ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ  |  θ = ½(ω₀ + ω)t
Four equations parallel to the linear kinematics big four — replace x with θ, v with ω, and a with α. Apply only when α is constant.
ω₀ — initial angular velocity (rad/s) ω — final angular velocity (rad/s) α — constant angular acceleration (rad/s²) θ — angular displacement (rad) t — time (s)
Where does this come from?

These are derived identically to the linear kinematic equations — integrate α = dω/dt once to get ω = ω₀ + αt, integrate again to get θ = ω₀t + ½αt². Eliminate t to get ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ, and average ω to get the last equation. Every linear kinematics trick (choosing which equation to use based on what's unknown) works the same way here.

Arc Length & Tangential Velocity
s = rθ     v_t = rω
Link between rotational and linear quantities for a point on the rim. A larger radius means greater linear speed for the same ω — this is why the outer edge of a spinning disk moves faster than the inner edge.
s — arc length (m) r — distance from rotation axis (m) θ — angle (rad) v_t — tangential speed (m/s) ω — angular velocity (rad/s)
Where does this come from?

s = rθ is just the definition of a radian rearranged. Differentiate both sides with respect to time: ds/dt = r·dθ/dt → v_t = rω. This is the tangential component of velocity (directed along the arc, perpendicular to r). Different points on a rigid body share the same ω but have different v_t depending on their r.

Tangential & Centripetal Acceleration
a_t = rα     a_c = ω²r = v²/r
A point on a rotating body has two acceleration components: tangential (changing speed) and centripetal (changing direction, always toward the center). They are perpendicular; total acceleration = √(a_t² + a_c²).
a_t — tangential acceleration (m/s²) a_c — centripetal acceleration (m/s²) r — radius (m) α — angular acceleration (rad/s²) ω — angular velocity (rad/s)
Where does this come from?

Differentiate v_t = rω with respect to time to get a_t = r·dω/dt = rα. The centripetal acceleration comes from the direction change of the velocity vector; for circular motion, a_c = v²/r = (rω)²/r = ω²r. Even at constant ω (α = 0), there is still centripetal acceleration because the direction of v is always changing — that's why circular motion requires a net inward force.

Torque

Torque Definition
τ = rF sinθ = r⊥F = rF⊥
Torque (N·m) is the rotational effect of a force. r is the distance from pivot to point of force application; θ is the angle between r and F. The lever arm r⊥ = r sinθ is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of force.
τ — torque (N·m) r — distance from pivot to force (m) F — force magnitude (N) θ — angle between r and F r⊥ = r sinθ — lever arm (m) F⊥ = F sinθ — perpendicular force component (N)
Where does this come from?

Only the component of force perpendicular to r actually causes rotation; the component parallel to r just pulls toward/away from the pivot and does nothing rotationally. So τ = r × (F sinθ) = rF⊥. Equivalently, you can extend the line of force and drop a perpendicular from the pivot to it — that perpendicular distance is the lever arm r⊥, and τ = r⊥·F. Both approaches give the same result: τ = rF sinθ.

Newton's 2nd Law for Rotation
Στ = Iα
Net torque equals moment of inertia times angular acceleration. The rotational analog of ΣF = ma — torque causes angular acceleration the same way force causes linear acceleration.
Στ — net torque about rotation axis (N·m) I — moment of inertia (kg·m²) α — angular acceleration (rad/s²)
Where does this come from?

For a single point mass m at radius r, the tangential force F_t = ma_t = m(rα). Its torque is τ = rF_t = mr²α. For a rigid body, sum over all particles: Στ = (Σmᵢrᵢ²)α = Iα. The quantity I = Σmᵢrᵢ² is the moment of inertia, which plays exactly the role that mass plays in F = ma — it measures resistance to angular acceleration.

Rotational Equilibrium
Στ = 0
When net torque about any point is zero, there is no angular acceleration. For full static equilibrium, both ΣF = 0 (no linear acceleration) and Στ = 0 (no angular acceleration) must hold simultaneously.
τ_cw — clockwise torques (take as negative) τ_ccw — counterclockwise torques (take as positive)
Where does this come from?

From Στ = Iα: if Στ = 0, then α = 0, meaning the object either stays at rest or rotates at constant ω. For static equilibrium we also need ΣF = 0. A key trick: you can take torques about any convenient pivot — choosing a point where an unknown force acts eliminates that unknown from the torque equation, simplifying the algebra significantly.

Moment of Inertia

Definition of I
I = Σmᵢrᵢ²
Moment of inertia (kg·m²) is the resistance to angular acceleration. It depends on both the total mass AND how that mass is distributed relative to the rotation axis — mass far from the axis contributes much more than mass close to it.
I — moment of inertia (kg·m²) mᵢ — mass of each particle (kg) rᵢ — distance of each particle from axis (m)
Where does this come from?

From the derivation of Στ = Iα (see above), I = Σmᵢrᵢ² naturally emerges as the "rotational mass." Because rᵢ is squared, mass far from the axis contributes disproportionately — doubling the radius quadruples the contribution to I. This is why a figure skater extends arms to slow down (I increases) and tucks them in to speed up (I decreases), even though their mass hasn't changed.

Common Shapes
Solid cylinder/disk: ½MR²  |  Hollow ring: MR²  |  Solid sphere: ⅖MR²  |  Hollow sphere: ⅔MR²  |  Rod (center): ¹⁄₁₂ML²  |  Rod (end): ⅓ML²
These are provided on the AP exam reference sheet, but knowing them helps you predict race-down-a-ramp outcomes. Smaller coefficient → smaller I → accelerates faster.
M — total mass of object (kg) R — radius (m) L — length of rod (m)
Where does this come from?

Each formula comes from integrating I = ∫r² dm over the object's geometry — calculus beyond AP 1, but the pattern makes sense. A hollow ring has all its mass at radius R, so I = MR². A solid disk has mass spread from r = 0 to R, averaging to ½MR². Moving mass closer to the axis (solid vs. hollow) always reduces I, which is why solid spheres beat hollow spheres rolling down a ramp.

Parallel Axis Theorem
I = I_cm + Md²
If you know the moment of inertia about an axis through the center of mass (I_cm), you can find I about any parallel axis a distance d away by adding Md². The CM axis always gives the minimum I.
I — moment of inertia about new axis (kg·m²) I_cm — moment of inertia about CM axis (kg·m²) M — total mass (kg) d — distance between the two parallel axes (m)
Where does this come from?

For an axis shifted by d from the CM, every particle's distance from the new axis gains a contribution from d. When you expand rᵢ² for the new axis and sum over all particles, cross terms involving the CM vanish (by definition of CM), leaving I = I_cm + Md². Classic application: a rod rotating about one end. I_end = I_cm + M(L/2)² = (1/12)ML² + (1/4)ML² = (1/3)ML² — matches the formula above.

Rotational Dynamics & Energy

Rotational KE
KE_rot = ½Iω²
Kinetic energy stored in a spinning object. Exactly analogous to ½mv² — replace mass with moment of inertia and speed with angular velocity.
KE_rot — rotational kinetic energy (J) I — moment of inertia (kg·m²) ω — angular velocity (rad/s)
Where does this come from?

Sum the kinetic energy of all particles: KE = Σ½mᵢvᵢ² = Σ½mᵢ(rᵢω)² = ½(Σmᵢrᵢ²)ω² = ½Iω². The derivation makes clear that ½Iω² is not a new formula — it is simply ½mv² applied to every piece of the rotating body and summed up.

Rolling Without Slipping
v_cm = Rω     a_cm = Rα
Constraint linking translational and rotational motion when an object rolls without slipping. The contact point is instantaneously at rest — the bottom of the wheel has zero velocity relative to the ground.
v_cm — speed of center of mass (m/s) R — radius of object (m) ω — angular velocity (rad/s) a_cm — linear acceleration of CM (m/s²) α — angular acceleration (rad/s²)
Where does this come from?

For pure rolling (no slipping), the arc length the wheel rotates through equals the distance the CM travels: s = Rθ. Differentiate once: v_cm = Rω. Differentiate again: a_cm = Rα. This constraint ties the two degrees of freedom together into one, which is why rolling problems can be solved with a single energy or force equation rather than separate translational and rotational equations.

Total KE (Rolling)
KE_total = ½mv² + ½Iω²
A rolling object has both translational KE (motion of the CM) and rotational KE (spinning about the CM). Both terms must be included — forgetting ½Iω² is one of the most common AP errors.
m — total mass (kg) v — speed of CM (m/s) I — moment of inertia about CM axis (kg·m²) ω — angular velocity (rad/s)
Where does this come from?

The kinetic energy theorem for a rigid body separates into two parts: KE of the CM's translation plus KE of rotation about the CM. These are independent — the body's mass moving as a whole gives ½mv_cm², and its spin about the CM gives ½I_cm·ω². For rolling, use v = Rω to express everything in terms of one variable before solving energy conservation equations.

Rolling Down an Incline
v = √( 2gh / (1 + I/mR²) )
Final speed at the bottom of a ramp of height h for an object rolling without slipping from rest. Objects with smaller I/mR² (like solid cylinders) reach the bottom faster than those with larger I/mR² (like hollow rings).
v — speed at bottom (m/s) g — 9.8 m/s² h — height of ramp (m) I — moment of inertia about CM (kg·m²) m — mass (kg), R — radius (m)
Where does this come from?

Apply energy conservation: mgh = ½mv² + ½Iω². Substitute the rolling constraint ω = v/R: mgh = ½mv² + ½I(v/R)² = ½v²(m + I/R²). Solving for v gives v = √(2gh/(1 + I/mR²)). The factor I/mR² is the key — it is dimensionless and equals ½ for a solid disk, 1 for a ring, ⅖ for a solid sphere. Larger I/mR² means more energy goes into rotation and less into translational KE, so the object is slower.

Angular Momentum

Angular Momentum
L = Iω     (point mass: L = mvr)
Rotational analog of linear momentum p = mv. For a point mass moving with speed v at perpendicular distance r from an axis, L = mvr. For an extended rigid body, L = Iω. Units: kg·m²/s.
L — angular momentum (kg·m²/s) I — moment of inertia (kg·m²) ω — angular velocity (rad/s) m — mass (kg), v — speed (m/s), r — perpendicular distance to axis (m)
Where does this come from?

For a point mass in circular motion, L = r × p = r·mv·sin90° = mvr (when v is perpendicular to r). For a rigid body, sum over all particles: L = Σmᵢrᵢ·vᵢ = Σmᵢrᵢ·(rᵢω) = (Σmᵢrᵢ²)ω = Iω. Angular momentum is a vector; for AP 1 problems, the magnitude L = Iω is usually sufficient, with sign indicating the sense of rotation.

Angular Impulse
τ_net · Δt = ΔL
Net torque applied for a time interval produces a change in angular momentum. Rotational analog of the impulse-momentum theorem J = FΔt = Δp.
τ_net — net torque (N·m) Δt — time interval (s) ΔL — change in angular momentum (kg·m²/s)
Where does this come from?

From Στ = Iα = I·Δω/Δt = ΔL/Δt. Multiply both sides by Δt: Στ·Δt = ΔL. Just as a large force applied briefly gives the same impulse as a small force applied for a long time, a large torque for a short time gives the same angular impulse as a small torque for a long time. The area under a τ-t graph equals ΔL.

Conservation of Angular Momentum
If Στ_ext = 0:   L_i = L_f   →   I_i ω_i = I_f ω_f
When there is no net external torque on a system, total angular momentum is conserved. Classic examples: spinning skater, collapsing star, diver going into a tuck. Pulling arms in decreases I, so ω must increase to keep L constant.
I_i, ω_i — initial moment of inertia and angular velocity I_f, ω_f — final moment of inertia and angular velocity
Where does this come from?

From angular impulse: if Στ_ext = 0 for the entire event, then ΔL = Στ_ext·Δt = 0, so L is constant. This is the rotational analog of conservation of linear momentum (which holds when net external force = 0). Note that kinetic energy is NOT generally conserved — a skater pulling her arms in gains KE because her muscles do work on her limbs. But L is still conserved because there is no external torque.

What AP Physics 1 Actually Tests

Exam focus Momentum problems are usually about applying conservation carefully, not just plugging numbers into formulas. The AP exam rewards clear direction choices, correct signs, and separating x/y components in 2D collisions.

Big Idea 1 — Momentum is directional

p = mv is a vector. In 1D, choose a positive direction and stick with it. In 2D, conserve momentum independently in x and y. Do not add vector components until after each component has been conserved.

Big Idea 2 — Impulse changes momentum

J = FΔt = Δp = mΔv. The impulse delivered by a force is the area under an F–t graph. A large force applied briefly can produce the same Δp as a small force applied longer.

Big Idea 3 — Conservation needs an isolated system

If no net external force acts, total momentum is constant: Σp_before = Σp_after. External forces include friction, gravity from outside the system, and contact with a wall.

Big Idea 4 — Collision type affects energy, not momentum

Momentum is conserved in isolated collisions whether elastic or inelastic. Kinetic energy is only conserved in elastic collisions. Perfectly inelastic collisions maximize KE loss when objects stick together.

Big Idea 5 — Recoil and explosions still conserve momentum

For an object at rest that explodes into pieces, total momentum remains zero. Pieces move in opposite directions and heavier pieces move slower so the momentum vector sum stays balanced.

Common AP Exam Traps

Trap 1 Forgetting momentum is a vector. Two objects moving in opposite directions can have net momentum near zero, even if each object has large speed.
Trap 2 Applying conservation when net external force is not zero. Friction, a wall, or any outside force breaks isolation.
Trap 3 Assuming elastic means momentum is not conserved. Momentum is always conserved in isolated collisions; elastic only means kinetic energy is also conserved.
Trap 4 Using the elastic velocity-swap formula when the target object is not initially at rest. That formula only works for one object initially stationary.
Trap 5 Sign errors in impulse problems. If a ball reverses direction, Δv = v_f − v_i includes the sign flip, so the impulse magnitude doubles if it bounces back.
Trap 6 Trying to conserve total momentum in two dimensions without splitting into components. You need separate x and y conservation equations.

Problem-Solving Frameworks

Collision / Conservation Problems

  1. Draw a diagram — label velocities, masses, and choose a positive direction.
  2. Define the system — decide which objects are included and whether external forces are negligible.
  3. Write momentum conservation — Σp_before = Σp_after. For 2D, write separate x and y equations.
  4. Identify collision type — elastic (use KE too), inelastic (use only momentum), perfectly inelastic (objects stick, one unknown final velocity).
  5. Check signs and units — make sure momentum values have the correct direction and final answers make physical sense.

Impulse Problems

  1. Choose a sign convention — define positive direction for velocities and forces.
  2. Compute impulse — J = ∫F dt or use simple area formulas: rectangle, triangle, trapezoid.
  3. Use Δp = J — relate impulse to momentum change: m(v_f − v_i) = J.
  4. Find final velocity — v_f = v_i + J/m, keeping the sign from the impulse.
  5. Interpret the result — decide whether the object sped up, slowed down, or reversed direction.

Must-Know Conceptual Scenarios

1D elastic collision with a stationary target

Use momentum and kinetic energy conservation together. For equal masses, the moving object stops and the target moves away with the incoming speed.

Perfectly inelastic collision

Objects stick together after collision. Use momentum only: m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = (m₁ + m₂)v_f. KE decreases and the missing energy becomes heat or deformation.

Explosion / recoil

A system at rest that breaks apart still has zero total momentum. Pieces move in opposite directions so their momenta cancel.

Ballistic pendulum

Phase 1: use momentum for the inelastic collision. Phase 2: use energy conservation for the rise. Never use energy during the collision phase.

2D collision component analysis

Write separate momentum equations for x and y. Angle signs matter: above the axis is positive, below is negative, and the two components conserve independently.

Impulse from a force–time graph

The impulse equals the area under the F–t curve, and that area equals Δp. Rectangle: J = FΔt. Triangle: J = ½F_maxΔt. Trapezoid: J = ½(F₁+F₂)Δt.

5-Point Exam Strategy

Free-response strategy Write the physics principle first, then substitute numbers. Show the momentum equation before solving, and label directions clearly to earn partial credit.

What earns full marks

  • State the conservation principle or impulse relation used.
  • Define positive direction and write signed velocities.
  • Show the general equation before plugging in numbers.
  • Explain why you can or cannot use kinetic energy conservation.
  • Include units and comment on the physical meaning when possible.

Practice Problems

Try each problem on paper first, then expand the answer to check your work and reasoning.

1 A 0.15 kg baseball is thrown at 40 m/s. A batter hits it back at 55 m/s in the opposite direction. The ball is in contact with the bat for 1.2 ms. Find (a) the impulse on the ball and (b) the average force the bat exerts on the ball.
Show Solution

Define "away from batter" as positive. v_i = +40 m/s, v_f = −55 m/s (toward pitcher).

(a) Impulse: J = Δp = m·Δv = 0.15 × (−55 − 40) = 0.15 × (−95) = −14.25 N·s

The magnitude is 14.25 N·s directed toward the pitcher.

(b) Average force: F_avg = J / Δt = −14.25 / (1.2 × 10⁻³) = −11 875 N ≈ −11.9 kN

This huge force highlights why batting helmets and padding matter — short contact times require enormous forces for the same impulse.

2 A 3 kg cart moving right at 6 m/s collides with a 5 kg cart moving left at 2 m/s. They stick together. Find (a) the final velocity and (b) the kinetic energy lost.
Show Solution

Right = positive. p_i = 3(6) + 5(−2) = 18 − 10 = 8 kg·m/s.

(a) (m₁ + m₂)v_f = 8 → v_f = 8/8 = +1.0 m/s (to the right)

(b) KE_i = ½(3)(6²) + ½(5)(2²) = 54 + 10 = 64 J

KE_f = ½(8)(1²) = 4 J

ΔKE = 4 − 64 = −60 J (60 J lost to deformation/heat)

3 A 0.5 kg block slides at 8 m/s on a frictionless track and has an elastic collision with a stationary 1.5 kg block. Find both final velocities.
Show Solution

Use elastic collision formulas (m₂ at rest):

v₁f = (m₁ − m₂)/(m₁ + m₂) · v₀ = (0.5 − 1.5)/(0.5 + 1.5) · 8 = (−1/2) · 8 = −4 m/s (bounces back)

v₂f = 2m₁/(m₁ + m₂) · v₀ = 2(0.5)/2.0 · 8 = 0.5 · 8 = +4 m/s

Check KE: KE_i = ½(0.5)(64) = 16 J. KE_f = ½(0.5)(16) + ½(1.5)(16) = 4 + 12 = 16 J ✓

Check momentum: p_i = 0.5(8) = 4. p_f = 0.5(−4) + 1.5(4) = −2 + 6 = 4 ✓

4 A 70 kg astronaut at rest pushes off a 200 kg satellite, giving the satellite a velocity of 0.5 m/s. Find the astronaut's recoil velocity and the kinetic energy that must have come from the push.
Show Solution

System starts at rest: p_total = 0. After explosion:

0 = 70·v_astro + 200·(0.5) → v_astro = −100/70 = −1.43 m/s (opposite to satellite)

KE generated = KE_f − KE_i = [½(70)(1.43²) + ½(200)(0.5²)] − 0

= [71.4 + 25] − 0 = 96.4 J

This energy came from the astronaut's muscles (chemical energy → kinetic). Momentum is conserved (net external = 0 in space) but KE increases.

5 A 0.02 kg bullet traveling at 300 m/s embeds in a 2 kg wooden block hanging from a 1.5 m string. How high does the block swing?
Show Solution

Phase 1 — Inelastic collision (momentum):

m_b · v₀ = (m_b + M) · v_f

0.02 × 300 = (0.02 + 2) · v_f → 6 = 2.02 · v_f → v_f = 2.97 m/s

Phase 2 — Pendulum swing (energy):

½(m_b + M)v_f² = (m_b + M)gh

h = v_f² / (2g) = (2.97)² / (2 × 9.8) = 8.82 / 19.6 = 0.45 m

Note: The string length (1.5 m) is extra information — it just tells you the block won't complete a full circle (0.45 m < 1.5 m, so it swings and stops).

FRQ Example — Annotated Scoring

How to read this Each green check shows a discrete scoring point the AP graders look for. The sample response below would earn full credit. Notice how each point is a separate sentence or step — graders give partial credit, so write distinct, labeled statements.

Situation: Two carts on a frictionless track. Cart A (mass 2 kg) moves right at 4 m/s. Cart B (mass 3 kg) is stationary. After the collision, Cart A moves right at 0.4 m/s.

Part (a): Find the velocity of Cart B after the collision. Is the collision elastic, inelastic, or perfectly inelastic? Justify your answer.

Part (b): Sketch a momentum bar chart showing p_A, p_B, and p_total before and after the collision.

Part (c): A student claims "Cart B exerts a larger force on Cart A than Cart A exerts on Cart B because Cart B is heavier." Is the student correct? Explain using Newton's laws.

Part (d): The experiment is repeated but now there is a small amount of friction on the track. Does Cart B's final speed increase, decrease, or stay the same compared to part (a)? Justify.

Part (a) — 3 points
EQUATION

Apply conservation of momentum (frictionless track = isolated system):

p_before = p_after
2(4) + 3(0) = 2(0.4) + 3·v_Bf
8 = 0.8 + 3·v_Bf → v_Bf = 7.2/3 = 2.4 m/s right

SUBSTITUTION

KE_before = ½(2)(4²) = 16 J. KE_after = ½(2)(0.4²) + ½(3)(2.4²) = 0.16 + 8.64 = 8.80 J.

ANSWER

KE is not conserved (8.80 J ≠ 16 J) and the carts did not stick together, so the collision is inelastic (not elastic, not perfectly inelastic).

Part (b) — 2 points

Momentum bar chart — bars must be proportional to values:

BEFORE:
p_A = +8 kg·m/s  |████████|
p_B =  0 kg·m/s  |        |
p_tot = +8 kg·m/s  |████████|

AFTER:
p_A = +0.8 kg·m/s  |█|
p_B = +7.2 kg·m/s  |███████|
p_tot = +8.0 kg·m/s  |████████|
KEY POINT

Total momentum bar is identical before and after — this visually demonstrates conservation. The individual bars change, but the total does not.

Part (c) — 2 points
ANSWER

The student is incorrect.

EXPLANATION

By Newton's Third Law, the forces that Cart A and Cart B exert on each other are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction — always, regardless of mass. The contact force F_AB = −F_BA at every instant during the collision. Mass does not affect the magnitude of the Newton's 3rd Law pair. (Note: The two carts DO have different accelerations — a = F/m — but the forces are equal.)

Part (d) — 2 points
ANSWER

Cart B's final speed would decrease.

REASONING

With friction present, the track exerts an external force on the system, so total momentum is no longer conserved — it decreases during the event. Less total momentum is available to distribute between the carts after the collision, so Cart B receives less momentum and has a lower final speed.

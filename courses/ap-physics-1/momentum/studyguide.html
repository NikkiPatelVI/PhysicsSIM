5 Big Ideas

1
Momentum is a vector quantity

p = mv always carries a direction. In 1D, assign positive and negative carefully before calculating. In 2D, treat x- and y-components independently — conservation holds in each direction separately.

2
Impulse equals change in momentum

J = FΔt = Δp = mΔv. Force and time are interchangeable — a large force for a short time can equal the same impulse as a small force over a long time. The area under an F-t graph is the impulse.

3
Conservation of momentum (isolated systems)

If no net external force acts, total momentum is constant: Σp_before = Σp_after. "External" means from outside the system. Internal forces (like collision forces between two objects) cancel by Newton's 3rd Law.

4
Collision types differ in KE — not momentum

Momentum is always conserved in collisions (when isolated). KE may or may not be. Elastic: ΔKE = 0. Perfectly inelastic: maximum KE lost, objects stick. Real collisions are in between — partially inelastic.

5
Center of mass moves at constant velocity (isolated system)

v_cm = p_total / M. In an isolated system, external forces are zero, so p_total is constant and v_cm is constant. Even during explosions or collisions, the center of mass keeps moving the same way.

6 Common Traps

Trap 1
Forgetting momentum is a vector

Two 2 kg blocks moving at 3 m/s in opposite directions have zero total momentum, not 12 kg·m/s. Always set up a sign convention and check directions before adding momenta.

Trap 2
Using conservation when it doesn't apply

Momentum is only conserved when net external force = 0. Gravity, friction with a fixed surface, or a wall hit all add external forces. Ask: is the system isolated? If an external force acts during the collision/event, momentum is NOT conserved.

Trap 3
Thinking KE is conserved in "elastic" collisions between real objects

True elastic collisions (perfect KE conservation) rarely occur in real life — they're an idealization. On the AP exam, "elastic" means you may use BOTH momentum AND KE conservation. "Inelastic" means use ONLY momentum conservation.

Trap 4
Mixing up elastic velocity-swap formulas

v₁f = (m₁−m₂)/(m₁+m₂)·v₁i only applies when m₂ is initially at rest. If both objects are moving before the collision, use the full momentum + KE equations (or relative velocity: v₁i − v₂i = −(v₁f − v₂f)).

Trap 5
Impulse sign errors in collisions

A ball bounces off a wall. If it hits at +5 m/s and rebounds at −5 m/s, Δv = −10 m/s (not 0). The direction reversal doubles the impulse compared to a ball that just sticks to the wall. Always compute Δp = p_f − p_i with signs.

Trap 6
2D: forgetting to split into components

In 2D collisions, you have two independent conservation equations: Σp_x,before = Σp_x,after AND Σp_y,before = Σp_y,after. Never add vectors before conserving. Resolve each velocity into x and y components first.

Must-Know Scenarios

Scenario 1 — 1D Elastic Collision (target at rest)

Setup: Block m₁ moving at v₀ hits stationary block m₂. Elastic collision.

Equations (both apply):

m₁v₀ = m₁v₁f + m₂v₂f   (momentum)
½m₁v₀² = ½m₁v₁f² + ½m₂v₂f²   (kinetic energy)

Shortcuts:

  • Equal masses: v₁f = 0, v₂f = v₀ (complete velocity swap)
  • m₁ ≫ m₂: m₁ barely slows, m₂ shoots off at ≈ 2v₀
  • m₁ ≪ m₂: m₁ bounces back at ≈ −v₀, m₂ barely moves

Key check: After solving, verify KE_before = KE_after.

Scenario 2 — 1D Perfectly Inelastic Collision

Setup: Block m₁ at v₁ collides with block m₂ at v₂. They stick together.

Equation:

m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = (m₁ + m₂)v_f

Common variants:

  • Head-on (v₂ negative): v_f may be positive, negative, or zero
  • One object at rest: v_f = m₁v₁ / (m₁ + m₂)
  • After finding v_f, use energy methods for subsequent motion (sliding, rising, etc.)

KE lost: ΔKE = KE_f − KE_i (always negative — energy is lost to heat/deformation).

Scenario 3 — Explosion / Recoil

Setup: Object initially at rest explodes into two pieces.

Equation:

0 = m₁v₁f + m₂v₂f   →   m₁v₁f = −m₂v₂f

Key insights:

  • Pieces must move in opposite directions
  • Heavier piece moves slower: |v₁f|/|v₂f| = m₂/m₁
  • KE increases (chemical → kinetic) — energy comes from internal source, but momentum is still conserved (net external = 0)
  • Gun recoil: bullet goes one way, gun goes the other
Scenario 4 — Ballistic Pendulum

Setup: Bullet (mass m) embeds in hanging block (mass M). Block rises height h.

Two-phase solution:

Phase 1 (collision) — momentum: mv₀ = (m+M)v_f
Phase 2 (swing) — energy: ½(m+M)v_f² = (m+M)gh

Combine to find bullet speed: v₀ = (m+M)/m · √(2gh)

Key trap: You cannot use energy conservation for Phase 1 — it is an inelastic collision! Use momentum for the collision, then energy for the swing.

Scenario 5 — 2D Collision

Setup: Object A moving along x-axis hits stationary Object B. They scatter at angles θ_A and θ_B below/above x-axis.

Two equations (x and y independently):

x: m_A·v_A0 = m_A·v_Af·cosθ_A + m_B·v_Bf·cosθ_B
y: 0 = m_A·v_Af·sinθ_A − m_B·v_Bf·sinθ_B

Sign rules: Angle above x-axis → positive y-component. Below → negative. The y-equation shows the two objects scatter symmetrically around the x-axis (y-momenta cancel).

If elastic: Add KE equation as a third equation — three equations for two unknowns (v_Af and v_Bf).

Scenario 6 — Impulse from a Force-Time Graph

Key fact: J = ∫F dt = area under F-t curve.

  • Rectangle: J = F · Δt
  • Triangle: J = ½ · F_max · Δt
  • Trapezoid: J = ½(F₁ + F₂) · Δt

Once J is found: Δp = J → v_f = v_i + J/m.

Typical AP question: Two different force profiles (large brief force vs. small long force) with the same area — same impulse → same Δp → same change in velocity.

Practice Problems

1 A 0.15 kg baseball is thrown at 40 m/s. A batter hits it back at 55 m/s in the opposite direction. The ball is in contact with the bat for 1.2 ms. Find (a) the impulse on the ball and (b) the average force the bat exerts on the ball.
Show Solution

Define "away from batter" as positive. v_i = +40 m/s, v_f = −55 m/s (toward pitcher).

(a) Impulse: J = Δp = m·Δv = 0.15 × (−55 − 40) = 0.15 × (−95) = −14.25 N·s

The magnitude is 14.25 N·s directed toward the pitcher.

(b) Average force: F_avg = J / Δt = −14.25 / (1.2 × 10⁻³) = −11 875 N ≈ −11.9 kN

This huge force highlights why batting helmets and padding matter — short contact times require enormous forces for the same impulse.

2 A 3 kg cart moving right at 6 m/s collides with a 5 kg cart moving left at 2 m/s. They stick together. Find (a) the final velocity and (b) the kinetic energy lost.
Show Solution

Right = positive. p_i = 3(6) + 5(−2) = 18 − 10 = 8 kg·m/s.

(a) (m₁ + m₂)v_f = 8 → v_f = 8/8 = +1.0 m/s (to the right)

(b) KE_i = ½(3)(6²) + ½(5)(2²) = 54 + 10 = 64 J

KE_f = ½(8)(1²) = 4 J

ΔKE = 4 − 64 = −60 J (60 J lost to deformation/heat)

3 A 0.5 kg block slides at 8 m/s on a frictionless track and has an elastic collision with a stationary 1.5 kg block. Find both final velocities.
Show Solution

Use elastic collision formulas (m₂ at rest):

v₁f = (m₁ − m₂)/(m₁ + m₂) · v₀ = (0.5 − 1.5)/(0.5 + 1.5) · 8 = (−1/2) · 8 = −4 m/s (bounces back)

v₂f = 2m₁/(m₁ + m₂) · v₀ = 2(0.5)/2.0 · 8 = 0.5 · 8 = +4 m/s

Check KE: KE_i = ½(0.5)(64) = 16 J. KE_f = ½(0.5)(16) + ½(1.5)(16) = 4 + 12 = 16 J ✓

Check momentum: p_i = 0.5(8) = 4. p_f = 0.5(−4) + 1.5(4) = −2 + 6 = 4 ✓

4 A 70 kg astronaut at rest pushes off a 200 kg satellite, giving the satellite a velocity of 0.5 m/s. Find the astronaut's recoil velocity and the kinetic energy that must have come from the push.
Show Solution

System starts at rest: p_total = 0. After explosion:

0 = 70·v_astro + 200·(0.5) → v_astro = −100/70 = −1.43 m/s (opposite to satellite)

KE generated = KE_f − KE_i = [½(70)(1.43²) + ½(200)(0.5²)] − 0

= [71.4 + 25] − 0 = 96.4 J

This energy came from the astronaut's muscles (chemical energy → kinetic). Momentum is conserved (net external = 0 in space) but KE increases.

5 A 0.02 kg bullet traveling at 300 m/s embeds in a 2 kg wooden block hanging from a 1.5 m string. How high does the block swing?
Show Solution

Phase 1 — Inelastic collision (momentum):

m_b · v₀ = (m_b + M) · v_f

0.02 × 300 = (0.02 + 2) · v_f → 6 = 2.02 · v_f → v_f = 2.97 m/s

Phase 2 — Pendulum swing (energy):

½(m_b + M)v_f² = (m_b + M)gh

h = v_f² / (2g) = (2.97)² / (2 × 9.8) = 8.82 / 19.6 = 0.45 m

Note: The string length (1.5 m) is extra information — it just tells you the block won't complete a full circle (0.45 m < 1.5 m, so it swings and stops).

Annotated FRQ — Walkthrough

Situation: Two carts on a frictionless track. Cart A (mass 2 kg) moves right at 4 m/s. Cart B (mass 3 kg) is stationary. After the collision, Cart A moves right at 0.4 m/s.

Part (a): Find the velocity of Cart B after the collision. Is the collision elastic, inelastic, or perfectly inelastic? Justify your answer.

Part (b): Sketch a momentum bar chart showing p_A, p_B, and p_total before and after the collision.

Part (c): A student claims "Cart B exerts a larger force on Cart A than Cart A exerts on Cart B because Cart B is heavier." Is the student correct? Explain using Newton's laws.

Part (d): The experiment is repeated but now there is a small amount of friction on the track. Does Cart B's final speed increase, decrease, or stay the same compared to part (a)? Justify.

Part (a) — 3 points
EQUATION

Apply conservation of momentum (frictionless track = isolated system):

p_before = p_after
2(4) + 3(0) = 2(0.4) + 3·v_Bf
8 = 0.8 + 3·v_Bf → v_Bf = 7.2/3 = 2.4 m/s right

SUBSTITUTION

KE_before = ½(2)(4²) = 16 J. KE_after = ½(2)(0.4²) + ½(3)(2.4²) = 0.16 + 8.64 = 8.80 J.

ANSWER

KE is not conserved (8.80 J ≠ 16 J) and the carts did not stick together, so the collision is inelastic (not elastic, not perfectly inelastic).

Part (b) — 2 points

Momentum bar chart — bars must be proportional to values:

BEFORE:
p_A = +8 kg·m/s  |████████|
p_B =  0 kg·m/s  |        |
p_tot = +8 kg·m/s  |████████|

AFTER:
p_A = +0.8 kg·m/s  |█|
p_B = +7.2 kg·m/s  |███████|
p_tot = +8.0 kg·m/s  |████████|
KEY POINT

Total momentum bar is identical before and after — this visually demonstrates conservation. The individual bars change, but the total does not.

Part (c) — 2 points
ANSWER

The student is incorrect.

EXPLANATION

By Newton's Third Law, the forces that Cart A and Cart B exert on each other are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction — always, regardless of mass. The contact force F_AB = −F_BA at every instant during the collision. Mass does not affect the magnitude of the Newton's 3rd Law pair. (Note: The two carts DO have different accelerations — a = F/m — but the forces are equal.)

Part (d) — 2 points
ANSWER

Cart B's final speed would decrease.

REASONING

With friction present, the track exerts an external force on the system, so total momentum is no longer conserved — it decreases during the event. Less total momentum is available to distribute between the carts after the collision, so Cart B receives less momentum and has a lower final speed.

5-Point Momentum Strategy

  1. Identify the system — list all objects involved. Ask: are there net external forces? If no → momentum is conserved.
  2. Set up a sign convention — pick a positive direction and label every velocity with the correct sign before writing equations.
  3. Identify collision type — elastic (use momentum + KE), inelastic (use only momentum), perfectly inelastic (they stick, use only momentum, one unknown v_f).
  4. For 2D: split into components — write two momentum equations (x and y). Solve the simpler equation first (usually y, where an object may have zero initial y-momentum).
  5. Check your answer — verify momentum is conserved numerically. If elastic, verify KE is conserved. Check signs (direction) make physical sense.

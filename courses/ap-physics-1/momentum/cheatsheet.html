Momentum & Impulse

Linear Momentum
p = mv
Momentum is a vector — direction matters. A heavy slow object can have the same momentum as a light fast one. SI unit: kg·m/s.
p — momentum (kg·m/s) m — mass (kg) v — velocity (m/s, vector)
Where does this come from?

Definition, introduced by Newton as "quantity of motion." The more precise form of Newton's 2nd Law is ΣF = dp/dt (rate of change of momentum), not ΣF = ma — the ma form only holds when mass is constant. Momentum is the fundamental quantity; force is the rate at which it changes.

Impulse
J = FΔt = Δp
Impulse is the product of net force and time interval. It equals the change in momentum — a vector. The area under a force-time graph is the impulse.
J — impulse (N·s = kg·m/s) F — average net force (N) Δt — time of force application (s) Δp = p_f − p_i — change in momentum
Where does this come from?

From Newton's 2nd Law: F = Δp/Δt. Multiply both sides by Δt: F·Δt = Δp. This is the impulse-momentum theorem. For varying forces, J = ∫F dt = area under F-t graph. Key insight: the same Δp (same impulse) can come from a large force for a short time or a small force for a long time — this is why airbags and crumple zones extend collision time to reduce peak force.

Impulse-Momentum Theorem
F_avg · Δt = m·Δv
The average force times the contact time equals the change in momentum. Use this when force is unknown but contact time and velocity change are given.
F_avg — average force during collision (N) Δt — collision time (s) Δv = v_f − v_i — change in velocity (m/s)
Where does this come from?

Same as J = Δp, rewritten. For constant mass: Δp = m·v_f − m·v_i = m·Δv. So F_avg·Δt = m·Δv. This is the AP exam's favourite form for collision problems: given initial and final velocity, find average force (divide by Δt) or collision time (divide by force). Always check signs — Δv is a vector!

Conservation of Momentum

Conservation Law
p_total,i = p_total,f
Total momentum of an isolated system is constant. "Isolated" means no external net force. Internal forces (object A on B, B on A) cancel by Newton's 3rd Law.
Applies when: ΣF_external = 0 m₁v₁i + m₂v₂i = m₁v₁f + m₂v₂f
Where does this come from?

From Newton's 3rd Law: when objects A and B interact, F_AB = −F_BA. Both act for the same time Δt: J_AB = −J_BA → Δp_A = −Δp_B → Δp_total = 0. Total momentum doesn't change. If there's a net external force (like friction or gravity with a non-normal component), momentum is not conserved — check this before applying the law.

2-Body Collision (1D)
m₁v₁i + m₂v₂i = m₁v₁f + m₂v₂f
General momentum conservation for two objects. Works for all collision types. Add a second equation (KE conserved or objects stick) to solve for two unknowns.
Subscript i — initial velocity Subscript f — final velocity Signs: rightward positive, leftward negative
Where does this come from?

Apply conservation of momentum: total p before = total p after. With two unknowns (v₁f and v₂f) you need a second equation. For elastic collisions: also conserve KE → gives relative velocity equation v₁f − v₂f = −(v₁i − v₂i). For perfectly inelastic: v₁f = v₂f = v_f (they stick), so you only have one unknown.

2D Momentum Conservation
Σp_x,i = Σp_x,f  ·  Σp_y,i = Σp_y,f
In 2D, momentum is conserved independently in x and y directions. Decompose all velocity vectors into components and apply conservation to each axis separately.
x-component: m₁v₁ₓ + m₂v₂ₓ before = same after y-component: m₁v₁ᵧ + m₂v₂ᵧ before = same after
Where does this come from?

Conservation of momentum is a vector law — it holds in every direction simultaneously. Decompose each momentum vector into x and y components and apply the scalar conservation law to each component independently. This gives you two equations for a 2D collision. If one object is initially at rest (target practice style), and you know the final angle of one object, you can find everything.

Collision Types

Elastic Collision
KE_i = KE_f  ·  p_i = p_f
Both momentum AND kinetic energy are conserved. Billiard balls, atomic collisions. Objects bounce off with no energy lost.
Special case equal masses: velocities swap v₁f = ((m₁−m₂)v₁i + 2m₂v₂i) / (m₁+m₂) v₂f = ((m₂−m₁)v₂i + 2m₁v₁i) / (m₁+m₂)
Where does this come from?

Solve the system of two equations: momentum conservation + KE conservation. The velocity formulas come from eliminating one unknown. Equal mass shortcut: m₁ = m₂ → v₁f = v₂i, v₂f = v₁i (velocities swap). If m₂ >> m₁ (wall): v₁f ≈ −v₁i (elastic bounce). If m₁ >> m₂ (bowling ball hits marble): v₁f ≈ v₁i, v₂f ≈ 2v₁i.

Perfectly Inelastic Collision
m₁v₁i + m₂v₂i = (m₁+m₂)v_f
Objects stick together after collision. Maximum kinetic energy is lost (but momentum is still conserved). Typical AP scenario: ballistic pendulum, clay.
v_f = (m₁v₁i + m₂v₂i) / (m₁+m₂) ΔKE = ½(m₁+m₂)v_f² − (½m₁v₁i² + ½m₂v₂i²)
Where does this come from?

The objects stick together → same final velocity v_f. Momentum conservation: m₁v₁i + m₂v₂i = (m₁+m₂)v_f. Solve for v_f. The kinetic energy lost = initial KE − final KE — this goes to deformation, heat, and sound. You can verify that KE is always lost in inelastic collisions (never gained), which follows from the center-of-mass frame analysis.

Explosion / Recoil
0 = m₁v₁f + m₂v₂f
System starts at rest → total momentum is zero before and after. The two fragments fly apart with equal and opposite momenta: m₁v₁ = −m₂v₂.
v₁f = −(m₂/m₁)v₂f Heavier fragment → slower recoil
Where does this come from?

System starts at rest → p_total,i = 0. By conservation: p_total,f = 0 = m₁v₁f + m₂v₂f → m₁v₁f = −m₂v₂f. The momenta are equal and opposite regardless of the explosion energy. This is why a rifle recoils when fired: the bullet goes forward, gun goes backward, total momentum = 0. Same reason rockets work in space — no air needed.

Center of Mass

Center of Mass Position
x_cm = (m₁x₁ + m₂x₂ + …) / M
Weighted average position, weighted by mass. The system behaves as if all mass is concentrated at x_cm when an external force is applied.
x_cm — center of mass position (m) M = Σm — total mass (kg) x_i — position of each object (m)
Where does this come from?

For a system of particles, Newton's 2nd Law for the whole system gives: ΣF_ext = M·a_cm. This requires defining a_cm as the acceleration of the mass-weighted average position. Integrating twice defines x_cm = Σ(m_i·x_i)/M. The center of mass moves as if all the mass were at that point with all external forces applied there.

CoM Velocity
v_cm = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂) / M = p_total / M
The center of mass velocity equals total momentum divided by total mass. For an isolated system, v_cm is constant — even during collisions.
v_cm — center of mass velocity (m/s) p_total — total momentum (kg·m/s) M — total mass (kg)
Where does this come from?

Differentiate x_cm: v_cm = dx_cm/dt = Σ(m_i·v_i)/M = p_total/M. For an isolated system, p_total is conserved → v_cm is constant throughout any collision or explosion. This is why the center of mass of a firework keeps traveling in a parabola even as the fragments scatter — the total momentum doesn't change.

CoM for Isolated System
ΣF_ext = 0 → v_cm = constant
With no external forces, the center of mass moves at constant velocity forever — even if the internal pieces collide, explode, or interact in any way.
Internal forces cannot change p_total External forces change v_cm
Where does this come from?

ΣF_ext = dp_total/dt = M·a_cm. If ΣF_ext = 0, then a_cm = 0 → v_cm = constant. This is conservation of momentum restated in terms of the CoM. Practical AP use: in an explosion from rest, the CoM remains stationary — the fragments always fly apart so that Σ(m_i·r_i) = 0 relative to the original position.

Key Values & Units

Units
Momentum p: kg·m/s = N·s
Impulse J: N·s = kg·m/s
(Same unit — impulse equals change in momentum)
Collision Summary
All collisions: momentum conserved
Elastic: KE also conserved
Perfectly inelastic: max KE lost, stick together
Inelastic: somewhere between — most real collisions
Sign Convention
Choose a positive direction and stick with it.
Rightward → positive velocity
Leftward → negative velocity
Wrong sign = wrong direction of momentum

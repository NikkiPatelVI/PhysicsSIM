What the AP Exam Tests

Exam focus Unit 7 accounts for roughly 10–15% of the AP Physics 1 exam. The exam almost never asks you to compute a period numerically. Instead, expect proportional reasoning ("if mass triples, what happens to T?"), energy questions at specific positions, and qualitative explanations of why amplitude doesn't affect period. FRQs often combine SHM with energy conservation.

Big Idea 1 — Amplitude Does Not Affect Period

This is the most tested conceptual fact in Unit 7. The period of a mass-spring system (T = 2π√(m/k)) contains no A. Neither does the pendulum period. Doubling the amplitude doubles the maximum speed and quadruples the total energy, but the period stays exactly the same. If a question asks "what changes the period?" — amplitude is never the answer.

Big Idea 2 — Use Energy, Not Kinematics

The acceleration in SHM is not constant (a = −ω²x changes as x changes), which means the five constant-acceleration kinematics equations are invalid. To find speed at any position, use conservation of energy: ½kA² = ½mv² + ½kx². At equilibrium (x = 0), all energy is KE; at the endpoints (x = ±A), all energy is spring PE.

Big Idea 3 — Pendulum Period Depends on L and g, Nothing Else

T = 2π√(L/g) has no mass and no amplitude. This is why a pendulum clock ticks at the same rate regardless of the bob's weight, and why it would tick differently on the Moon. "What happens to the period if you move this pendulum to Jupiter?" → T decreases because g is larger. "What if you make the bob heavier?" → nothing changes.

Big Idea 4 — Restoring Force Always Points Toward Equilibrium

The defining feature of SHM is a restoring force proportional to displacement: F = −kx. At the positive endpoint, F is negative (pointing left/back). At the negative endpoint, F is positive (pointing right/back). At equilibrium, F = 0. This means force and acceleration are maximum at the endpoints, zero at equilibrium — exactly opposite to the velocity pattern.

Big Idea 5 — Period and Frequency Are Inverses

f = 1/T and T = 1/f. If the period doubles, the frequency is cut in half. If the mass quadruples on a spring, the period doubles (T ∝ √m), so the frequency is cut in half. This inverse relationship is tested often in conjunction with proportional reasoning — state the effect on T first, then invert to get the effect on f.

Common Exam Traps

Trap 1 — Using kinematics equations in SHM Since acceleration is not constant in SHM, equations like v² = v₀² + 2ax are invalid. If you need to find the speed at a specific position, use energy conservation: ½kA² = ½mv² + ½kx². If you need speed at equilibrium, set x = 0 and solve for v. Never plug into a kinematics equation mid-oscillation.
Trap 2 — Thinking amplitude changes the period Stretching a spring further or pulling a pendulum to a larger angle does NOT change the period (for small angles). It only changes the maximum speed and total energy. This surprises students because intuitively a larger amplitude "seems like it should take longer." The math shows otherwise — and the AP exam tests this directly.
Trap 3 — Forgetting the negative sign in F = −kx Hooke's Law is F = −kx, not F = kx. The negative sign is the physics — it means the force opposes the displacement. If you write F = kx (positive), you get a force that pushes the object further away from equilibrium, which is an unstable equilibrium (like a ball on top of a hill), not SHM. Always include the negative sign when analyzing direction of force or acceleration.
Trap 4 — Confusing period with half-period in word problems A pendulum clock ticks once per half-cycle (one side to the other). If the clock ticks every 1 second, the period is 2 seconds — it takes 2 seconds to complete a full back-and-forth cycle. Read carefully: "from one side to the other" = T/2; "back to starting position" = T.
Trap 5 — Applying pendulum formula to a spring (or vice versa) T = 2π√(L/g) is only for pendulums. T = 2π√(m/k) is only for mass-spring systems. Mass matters for springs but not pendulums. Gravity matters for pendulums but not springs (a spring oscillates identically horizontally or vertically once equilibrium is accounted for).

Problem-Solving Framework

Finding Speed at Any Position (Spring-Mass)

  1. Identify the amplitude A — this is the maximum displacement from equilibrium. If the object starts from rest at a stretched position, that position is A.
  2. Write energy conservation — ½kA² = ½mv² + ½kx², where x is the position you want the speed at.
  3. Substitute and solve for v — rearrange: v = √(k/m) · √(A² − x²). At x = 0 (equilibrium), v = A√(k/m) = v_max. At x = A (endpoint), v = 0.
  4. Check sign — speed is always positive; the direction of motion must be determined from context (which half of the oscillation is the object in?).

Proportional Reasoning for Period

  1. Identify the system — spring-mass (T = 2π√(m/k)) or pendulum (T = 2π√(L/g)).
  2. Identify what changes — which variable in the formula is being scaled?
  3. Find the scaling factor — since T ∝ √(variable), if the variable multiplies by n, T multiplies by √n.
  4. State the effect on f — frequency changes by the inverse factor. If T doubles, f halves.

Pendulum Energy Problems

  1. Find the height h — use geometry: h = L(1 − cosθ), where θ is the angle from vertical at the release point.
  2. Set up energy conservation — mgh = ½mv² (mass cancels). Solve: v = √(2gh).
  3. Use v_max at the bottom — the pendulum is fastest at the lowest point, where all energy is kinetic.

Must-Know Scenarios

Mass Released from Rest at Amplitude

The object starts at x = A with v = 0. All energy is spring PE: E = ½kA². It accelerates toward equilibrium, reaching maximum speed v_max = A√(k/m) at x = 0. It then decelerates, stopping at x = −A. One full oscillation takes T = 2π√(m/k). The motion is symmetric: left half mirrors right half.

Effect of Changing Amplitude Mid-Oscillation

If extra mass is added when the spring passes through equilibrium (v = v_max, x = 0), the new amplitude changes but the new period T' = 2π√((m+Δm)/k) is longer. If mass is added at the endpoint (v = 0), the period also changes but the amplitude stays the same — the spring PE is unchanged and now accelerates more mass, so the new v_max is smaller.

Pendulum on Different Planets

T = 2π√(L/g). On the Moon (g = 1.6 m/s²), T is about 2.5× longer than on Earth — the clock runs slow. On Jupiter (g ≈ 26 m/s²), T is about 1.6× shorter — the clock runs fast. To keep the same period, the length must scale proportionally with g: L/g = constant → if g doubles, L must double to maintain the same period.

Spring PE → Kinetic Energy Conversion

At position x, the fraction of total energy that is kinetic is (A² − x²)/A². At the quarter-amplitude position (x = A/2), KE = ½k(A² − A²/4) = (3/4)·½kA² — so 75% of total energy is kinetic. Spring PE is only 25% there. This type of "what fraction" question uses the energy equation directly.

Stretched Cable / Longer Pendulum Runs Slow

If a pendulum cable stretches, L increases → T increases → the clock ticks less frequently → it counts fewer seconds → the clock runs behind (slow). This is a classic AP word problem. The key: a longer pendulum has a longer period, so each "tick" takes more real time than the clock thinks.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Period Proportional Reasoning

A 0.5 kg mass on a spring of constant 50 N/m oscillates with a period T. (a) What happens to T if the mass is replaced with a 2 kg mass? (b) What happens to T if the original mass is kept but the spring constant is increased to 200 N/m? (c) What happens to the maximum speed if the amplitude is doubled?

Show solution

(a) T = 2π√(m/k). Mass increases by factor 4. Since T ∝ √m, T increases by √4 = 2. New period is 2T.

(b) k increases by factor 4. Since T ∝ 1/√k, T decreases by √4 = 2. New period is T/2.

(c) v_max = Aω = A√(k/m). Amplitude doubles → v_max doubles. Period is unchanged.

Problem 2 — Speed at a Specific Position

A 0.5 kg cart on a spring (k = 50 N/m) is released from rest at x = 0.10 m from equilibrium. Find: (a) the maximum speed of the cart, and (b) the speed when the cart is at x = 0.06 m.

Show solution

Given: A = 0.10 m, k = 50 N/m, m = 0.5 kg.

(a) At equilibrium (x = 0): ½kA² = ½mv_max²

v_max = A√(k/m) = 0.10 × √(50/0.5) = 0.10 × 10 = 1.0 m/s.

(b) Energy conservation at x = 0.06 m:

½(50)(0.10²) = ½(0.5)v² + ½(50)(0.06²)

0.25 = 0.25v² + 0.09 → 0.25v² = 0.16 → v = √0.64 = 0.80 m/s.

Problem 3 — Pendulum on the Moon

A pendulum clock is calibrated to keep perfect time on Earth (g = 9.8 m/s²). It is then taken to the Moon (g = 1.6 m/s²). (a) By what factor does its period change? (b) If the clock reads 12 hours elapsed, how much time has actually passed on the Moon?

Show solution

(a) T ∝ 1/√g. The ratio: T_Moon/T_Earth = √(g_Earth/g_Moon) = √(9.8/1.6) = √6.125 ≈ 2.47. The period is about 2.47 times longer on the Moon.

(b) Each tick of the clock takes 2.47× longer in real time. So when the clock says 12 hours have passed, the actual elapsed time is 12 × 2.47 ≈ 29.6 hours. The clock runs slow on the Moon.

Problem 4 — Energy Fraction

A spring-mass system oscillates with amplitude A = 0.20 m and spring constant k = 80 N/m. At position x = 0.10 m, what fraction of the total mechanical energy is (a) spring potential energy, and (b) kinetic energy?

Show solution

Total energy: E = ½kA² = ½(80)(0.20²) = 1.60 J.

Spring PE at x = 0.10 m: PE = ½kx² = ½(80)(0.10²) = 0.40 J.

(a) Fraction as PE = 0.40/1.60 = 1/4 (25%). Note: x = A/2 → PE/E = x²/A² = (1/2)² = 1/4. Always true.

(b) Fraction as KE = 1 − 1/4 = 3/4 (75%). KE = 1.20 J.

Problem 5 — Mass Added at Endpoint

A 1.0 kg mass oscillates on a spring (k = 40 N/m) with amplitude 0.15 m. At the moment the mass reaches its maximum displacement, a 1.0 kg lump of clay is dropped onto it and sticks. (a) What is the new amplitude? (b) What is the new period? (c) What is the new maximum speed?

Show solution

At the endpoint, v = 0, so the clay lands on a stationary mass — no momentum change, spring is still stretched 0.15 m.

(a) The spring is still compressed by 0.15 m and both masses are at rest. The new amplitude is 0.15 m (unchanged — the spring's stored PE is unchanged).

(b) New mass = 2.0 kg. T' = 2π√(m'/k) = 2π√(2.0/40) = 2π√(0.05) ≈ 1.40 s. Old T = 2π√(1.0/40) ≈ 0.99 s. Period increased by √2.

(c) v_max = A√(k/m') = 0.15√(40/2.0) = 0.15 × √20 ≈ 0.67 m/s. (Old v_max was 0.15√40 ≈ 0.95 m/s — slower now because more mass.)

Annotated FRQ Example

Question Setup

A 0.4 kg block is attached to a horizontal spring (k = 100 N/m) on a frictionless surface. The block is pulled 0.12 m from equilibrium and released from rest. A motion detector records its position over time.

Part (a) — Calculate the period and frequency of oscillation

1 pt

Correct formula stated: T = 2π√(m/k)

1 pt

Correct substitution and answer: T = 2π√(0.4/100) = 2π(0.0632) ≈ 0.397 s. Frequency f = 1/T ≈ 2.52 Hz.

Part (b) — Find the maximum speed of the block

1 pt

Correct energy conservation equation: ½kA² = ½mv_max² (or v_max = A√(k/m)).

1 pt

Correct answer: v_max = 0.12√(100/0.4) = 0.12 × 15.8 ≈ 1.90 m/s.

Part (c) — At what position(s) is the block's speed equal to half its maximum speed?

1 pt

Energy conservation set up correctly: ½kA² = ½mv² + ½kx², with v = v_max/2.

1 pt

Solving: ½kx² = ½kA² − ½m(v_max/2)² = ½kA² − ⅛mv_max² = ½kA²(1 − ¼) = (3/4)·½kA² → x² = (3/4)A² → x = ±(√3/2)A ≈ ±0.104 m. Two positions stated.

Part (d) — The amplitude is now doubled. Describe what happens to the period and maximum speed.

1 pt

Period is unchanged — T = 2π√(m/k) has no amplitude. Explicit statement required.

1 pt

Maximum speed doubles — v_max = Aω, and A doubles while ω is unchanged. Justification citing v_max ∝ A earns the point.

5-Point FRQ Strategy

Free-response approach AP FRQs award partial credit generously. Write the correct formula first, substitute values second, solve third. A correct formula with an arithmetic mistake still earns most of the points.

What earns full marks

  • State which formula you're using and why (e.g., "Using energy conservation because acceleration is not constant in SHM")
  • Show the general equation before substituting numbers
  • For proportional reasoning: write the ratio explicitly (T'/T = √(m'/m)), don't just state the answer
  • When asked about amplitude's effect on period: explicitly say "amplitude does not appear in T = 2π√(m/k), so the period is unchanged"
  • Include units in every numerical answer

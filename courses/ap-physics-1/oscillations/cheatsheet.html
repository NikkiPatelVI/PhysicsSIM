Period & Frequency

Period–Frequency Relationship
T = 1/f     f = 1/T
Period T is the time for one complete oscillation (s). Frequency f is the number of complete oscillations per second (Hz). They are exact reciprocals — doubling T cuts f in half.
T — period (s) f — frequency (Hz = 1/s)
Where does this come from?

By definition, frequency is the number of cycles per unit time and period is the duration of one cycle. If you complete f cycles per second, each cycle takes 1/f seconds. This inverse relationship holds for every periodic phenomenon — waves, orbits, AC circuits. On the AP exam, the most common trap is forgetting to convert: if the problem gives period in seconds, frequency is in Hz, not in seconds.

Angular Frequency
ω = 2π/T = 2πf = √(k/m)
Angular frequency ω (rad/s) is the rate of oscillation in radians per second. It converts the regular frequency into the "rotational speed" of the equivalent circular motion that generates the sinusoidal oscillation.
ω — angular frequency (rad/s) T — period (s) f — frequency (Hz) k — spring constant (N/m) m — mass (kg)
Where does this come from?

SHM can be modeled as the horizontal projection of uniform circular motion. One full revolution is 2π radians, completed in time T, so angular speed = 2π/T. The formula ω = √(k/m) comes from solving the differential equation for a spring-mass system: ma = −kx → ω² = k/m. Knowing ω immediately gives T = 2π/ω and f = ω/(2π) without separate memorization.

Position in SHM
x(t) = A cos(ωt)    [released from amplitude]
Position oscillates between +A and −A. Starting from the amplitude (maximum displacement), position follows a cosine. Starting from equilibrium with maximum speed, it follows a sine. Amplitude A does not affect T or f.
x — displacement from equilibrium (m) A — amplitude (m) ω — angular frequency (rad/s) t — time (s)
Where does this come from?

Solving ẍ = −ω²x (Newton's second law for a spring) gives x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ), where φ is the initial phase. If released from rest at x = A, then φ = 0, giving x = A cos(ωt). The fact that amplitude A appears only as a scaling factor — not inside the cosine — is why amplitude doesn't change the period. This is the defining property of SHM and distinguishes it from nonlinear oscillations.

Mass-Spring System

Hooke's Law (Restoring Force)
F = −kx
The spring exerts a restoring force proportional to displacement and directed toward equilibrium. The negative sign means: displaced right → force acts left, and vice versa. This is what makes the motion oscillatory.
F — restoring force (N) k — spring constant (N/m) x — displacement from equilibrium (m)
Where does this come from?

Hooke's Law is empirical — it describes the linear region of spring behavior before the spring is permanently deformed. The negative sign is physics: for SHM to occur, the force must always point back toward equilibrium (restoring force). Any force of the form F = −kx, regardless of the physical mechanism, will produce SHM. This includes the net force on a pendulum for small angles, where the restoring component is −mg sinθ ≈ −(mg/L)x.

Period of Mass-Spring
T = 2π√(m/k)
Period depends on mass and spring constant only — NOT on amplitude. Heavier mass → longer period (more inertia to overcome). Stiffer spring → shorter period (stronger restoring force). The 2π comes from one full cycle of the equivalent circular motion.
T — period (s) m — mass (kg) k — spring constant (N/m)
Where does this come from?

From F = ma and F = −kx: ma = −kx → a = −(k/m)x. Comparing with a = −ω²x gives ω² = k/m → ω = √(k/m). Then T = 2π/ω = 2π√(m/k). The key insight: amplitude cancels entirely during the derivation, which is why it doesn't appear in the period formula. On the AP exam, the most common proportional-reasoning question is "if mass quadruples, T ______" — answer: doubles (T ∝ √m).

Spring Potential Energy
PE_spring = ½kx²
Elastic potential energy stored in a spring stretched or compressed by x from equilibrium. Maximum at the endpoints (x = ±A); zero at equilibrium. Always positive — it doesn't matter which direction the spring is displaced.
PE_spring — elastic potential energy (J) k — spring constant (N/m) x — displacement from equilibrium (m)
Where does this come from?

Work done by a variable force equals the area under the F vs. x graph. Since F = kx (taking magnitude), the graph is a triangle with area ½ · (kx) · x = ½kx². Alternatively, integrate: W = ∫₀ˣ kx' dx' = ½kx². This work is stored as potential energy. The x² dependence means that doubling the amplitude stores four times the energy — which is why the maximum speed (where all PE becomes KE) also depends on amplitude: v_max = A√(k/m).

Energy in SHM

Energy Conservation in SHM
½kA² = ½mv² + ½kx²
Total mechanical energy is constant (no friction). At the amplitude (x = A), all energy is spring PE and v = 0. At equilibrium (x = 0), all energy is KE and speed is maximum. Use this — not kinematics — to find speed at any position.
A — amplitude (m) k — spring constant (N/m) m — mass (kg) v — speed at position x (m/s) x — displacement from equilibrium (m)
Where does this come from?

Conservation of mechanical energy: E_total = KE + PE_spring = ½mv² + ½kx² = constant. At x = A (turning point), v = 0, so E_total = ½kA². This total never changes (no friction), so ½kA² = ½mv² + ½kx² at every point. Solving for v: v = √(k/m) · √(A² − x²) = ω√(A² − x²). This confirms v is maximum at x = 0 (v_max = Aω) and zero at x = ±A, which matches the position equation x = A cos(ωt).

Maximum Speed in SHM
v_max = A√(k/m) = Aω
The object moves fastest when passing through equilibrium (x = 0), where all energy is kinetic. Maximum speed is proportional to amplitude — twice the amplitude means twice the max speed. Doubling the mass halves the max speed.
v_max — maximum speed (m/s) A — amplitude (m) k — spring constant (N/m) m — mass (kg) ω = √(k/m) — angular frequency (rad/s)
Where does this come from?

From energy conservation: at x = 0, all energy is kinetic: ½mv_max² = ½kA². Solving: v_max = A√(k/m) = Aω. Notice that v_max does depend on amplitude (unlike T). This makes sense: a larger amplitude means the object travels farther in the same period, so it must go faster. The AP exam sometimes asks how v_max changes when amplitude changes — it scales linearly with A, while T does not change at all.

Force & Acceleration in SHM
F = −kx     a = −(k/m)x = −ω²x
Force and acceleration are maximum at the endpoints (x = ±A) and zero at equilibrium. They are always directed toward equilibrium (opposite to displacement). Since acceleration is not constant, kinematics equations DO NOT apply — use energy methods instead.
F — net force (N) a — acceleration (m/s²) k — spring constant (N/m) x — displacement from equilibrium (m) ω² = k/m
Where does this come from?

F = ma combined with F = −kx gives a = −(k/m)x. Because acceleration depends on position (which is constantly changing), it is not constant — so the five kinematics equations (v = v₀ + at, etc.) are invalid for SHM. The acceleration is in phase with the force: when the spring is fully stretched (max x), acceleration and force point back toward center with maximum magnitude. At equilibrium, acceleration is zero — the object is at maximum speed and changing direction of acceleration from inward to outward (or vice versa).

Pendulum

Period of a Simple Pendulum
T = 2π√(L/g)
Period depends on string length and gravitational field strength only — NOT on mass or amplitude (for small angles). Longer pendulum → longer period. Stronger gravity → shorter period. A pendulum on the Moon swings more slowly than on Earth.
T — period (s) L — length from pivot to center of mass of bob (m) g — gravitational field strength (9.8 m/s² on Earth)
Where does this come from?

For small angles (θ ≲ 15°), the restoring force on the pendulum bob is F ≈ −(mg/L)x, which has the form F = −kx with an effective spring constant k_eff = mg/L. Substituting into T = 2π√(m/k_eff) = 2π√(m·L/mg) = 2π√(L/g). The mass cancels — just like free fall. The small-angle approximation sinθ ≈ θ is what makes the restoring force linear (SHM). For large amplitudes, the period is slightly longer than 2π√(L/g) and does depend on amplitude.

Pendulum Energy
E = mgh_max = ½mv_max²
At maximum displacement, all energy is gravitational PE (h measured from lowest point). At the bottom, all energy is kinetic. Use h = L(1 − cosθ) to find the height of the bob above its lowest point given the angle θ.
h_max — maximum height above lowest point (m) L — pendulum length (m) θ — maximum angle from vertical h = L(1 − cosθ)
Where does this come from?

The bob traces an arc; its height above the lowest point is h = L − L cosθ = L(1 − cosθ). This comes from the geometry: the pivot is L above the lowest point; the bob hangs at vertical distance L cosθ below the pivot, so its height above the bottom is L − L cosθ. At the top of the swing: E = mgh = mgL(1 − cosθ). At the bottom: E = ½mv_max². Setting equal and solving for v_max: v_max = √(2gL(1 − cosθ)).

Comparing Spring vs. Pendulum
Spring: T = 2π√(m/k)     Pendulum: T = 2π√(L/g)
Spring period increases with mass; pendulum period is independent of mass. Spring period is independent of amplitude; pendulum period is also independent of amplitude (for small angles). Both use energy conservation to find speed at any position.
Spring: T ∝ √m (mass matters) Spring: T ∝ 1/√k (stiffer → faster) Pendulum: T ∝ √L (longer → slower) Pendulum: T ∝ 1/√g (stronger gravity → faster)
Where does this come from?

Both systems are SHM with different effective spring constants: for the mass-spring, k_eff = k; for the pendulum, k_eff = mg/L. The mass cancels in the pendulum case because the restoring force mg sinθ is itself proportional to mass — so doubling the mass doubles both the restoring force and the inertia, leaving T unchanged. This is the same reason all objects fall at the same rate: gravity scales with mass in exactly the right way to cancel mass from the motion equations.

m → 4m: T × 2 (spring only)
k → 4k: T ÷ 2 (spring only)
L → 4L: T × 2 (pendulum only)
A → 2A: T unchanged, v_max × 2
g → 4g: T ÷ 2 (pendulum only)

Fluid Properties

Absolute vs. Gauge Pressure
P_gauge = P − P_atm
Absolute pressure is the full pressure including atmosphere. Gauge pressure is the excess above atmospheric — what most instruments read. At the open surface, gauge pressure = 0.
P — absolute pressure (Pa) P_atm ≈ 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa P_gauge — excess above atmosphere (Pa)
Density
ρ = m / V
Mass per unit volume. For water, ρ ≈ 1000 kg/m³.
ρ — density (kg/m³) m — mass (kg) V — volume (m³)
Pressure
P = F / A
Force per unit area. Pressure acts equally in all directions in a static fluid.
P — pressure (Pa = N/m²) F — force (N) A — area (m²)
Gauge Pressure / Hydrostatic Pressure
P = P₀ + ρgh
Pressure at depth h below the surface. P₀ is the pressure at the surface (often atmospheric, ~101 325 Pa).
P₀ — surface pressure (Pa) ρ — fluid density (kg/m³) g — 9.8 m/s² h — depth below surface (m)

Pascal's & Archimedes' Principles

Pascal's Principle
F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂
Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid transmits equally throughout. Basis of hydraulic systems. Work is conserved: the small piston travels farther — F₁d₁ = F₂d₂.
F₁, F₂ — input/output forces (N) A₁, A₂ — piston areas (m²)
Buoyant Force (Archimedes)
F_b = ρ_f · V_sub · g
Upward force on a submerged/floating object equals the weight of fluid displaced.
F_b — buoyant force (N) ρ_f — fluid density (kg/m³) V_sub — submerged volume (m³) g — 9.8 m/s²
Floating Condition
ρ_obj / ρ_f = V_sub / V_obj
Fraction of object submerged equals the ratio of densities. Object floats when ρ_obj < ρ_f.
ρ_obj — object density (kg/m³) ρ_f — fluid density (kg/m³)

Fluid Flow

Volume Flow Rate
Q = A · v
Volume of fluid passing a cross-section per second. Units: m³/s.
Q — flow rate (m³/s) A — cross-sectional area (m²) v — fluid speed (m/s)
Continuity Equation
A₁v₁ = A₂v₂
For an incompressible fluid in a pipe, flow rate is constant. Smaller area → faster speed.
A₁, A₂ — areas at points 1, 2 (m²) v₁, v₂ — speeds at points 1, 2 (m/s)
Bernoulli's Equation
P + ρgy + ½ρv² = const
Energy conservation per unit volume for ideal fluid flow. Higher speed → lower pressure (and vice versa). y is height above a chosen reference level.
P — pressure (Pa) ρgy — gravitational PE density (Pa) ½ρv² — kinetic energy density (Pa)
Bernoulli (two points)
P₁ + ρgy₁ + ½ρv₁² = P₂ + ρgy₂ + ½ρv₂²
Apply between any two points along a streamline in steady, incompressible, non-viscous flow. y is height above a reference — pick the lower point as y = 0.
Subscripts 1, 2 — two points on streamline y₁, y₂ — heights above reference (m)
Torricelli's Theorem
v = √(2gy_A)
Exit speed at a hole when the fluid surface (point A) is a height y_A above the hole. Derived from Bernoulli: both pressures are P_atm and cancel; v_A ≈ 0 by continuity (large tank).
v — exit speed at hole (m/s) y_A — height of fluid surface above hole (m)

Quick Reference: Units & Constants

ρ_air ≈ 1.2 kg/m³
P_atm = 101 325 Pa ≈ 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa
1 atm = 101 325 Pa = 760 mmHg
SI Units

What AP Physics 1 Actually Tests

Exam focus Fluid dynamics questions are heavily conceptual — qualitative reasoning about pressure, flow, and buoyancy usually earns as many points as calculation. Don't just memorize formulas; know what each term means physically.

Big Idea 1 — Pressure increases with depth

P = P₀ + ρgh is linear in h. Two points at the same depth in a connected fluid are at the same pressure — even if the containers have different shapes. This is why a U-tube manometer works.

Big Idea 2 — Continuity means speed and area trade off

A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ is conservation of mass for incompressible flow. The fluid doesn't pile up anywhere. A narrower pipe → faster flow. A wider pipe → slower flow. Sketch the pipe and label what's bigger/smaller first.

Big Idea 3 — Bernoulli is energy conservation

P + ½ρv² + ρgh = constant is just conservation of energy per unit volume. If speed goes up (kinetic energy density up), pressure must go down. This explains lift on a wing, flow in a Venturi meter, and why a shower curtain blows inward.

Big Idea 4 — Buoyancy depends on displaced volume, not object mass

F_b = ρ_fluid · V_displaced · g. The object's weight doesn't appear in the buoyant force formula — only the volume of fluid it displaces matters. A hollow steel ball and a solid plastic ball of the same outer volume experience the same buoyant force.

Common AP Exam Traps

Trap 1 Confusing absolute pressure with gauge pressure. Gauge pressure = P − P_atm. A tire gauge reads gauge pressure. Bernoulli's equation uses absolute pressure.
Trap 2 Applying Bernoulli where it doesn't apply. Bernoulli requires: steady flow, incompressible fluid, non-viscous fluid, along a single streamline. Turbulent flow, viscous fluids (honey), and compressible flow (gas at high speed) violate these.
Trap 3 Thinking a heavier object sinks faster. Objects sink or float based on density comparison, not mass alone. A massive cruise ship floats; a small pebble sinks. Compare ρ_obj vs ρ_fluid.
Trap 4 Forgetting that pressure is a scalar. It acts in all directions. Don't apply a direction to pressure itself — only to the force it produces (F = PA, perpendicular to a surface).
Trap 5 — Doubling pressure doesn't double exit speed In v = √(2P_gauge/ρ), exit speed scales as the square root of source pressure. Doubling P_gauge multiplies v by √2 ≈ 1.41, not 2. Likewise, quadrupling P_gauge is required to double exit speed. Anything under a square root scales sub-linearly — a common FRQ trap.
Trap 6 — Depth doesn't change buoyant force on a fully submerged object F_b = ρ_fluid · V_submerged · g depends only on the volume displaced, not on how deep the object sits. Lowering a fully submerged object deeper does not increase buoyancy because V_submerged is unchanged.
Trap 7 — Sealed-top pressure can be below atmospheric When a finger seals the top of a straw containing water, the trapped air can be at less than atmospheric pressure. At the bottom opening (exposed to air) P = P_atm; working upward with P_top = P_atm − ρgh gives P_top < P_atm. Don't assume any trapped gas must be ≥ atmospheric.

Problem-Solving Frameworks

Buoyancy Problems

  1. Draw a free-body diagram — weight (mg down) and buoyant force (F_b up). Add tension or normal force if needed.
  2. Identify V_displaced — if fully submerged, V_displaced = V_object. If floating, V_displaced < V_object: the submerged fraction equals the density ratio (ρ_obj/ρ_fluid), which is the floating condition.
  3. Apply F_b = ρ_fluid · V_displaced · g — use the fluid's density, not the object's.
  4. Apply Newton's 2nd law — ΣF = ma. For equilibrium (floating, hovering): ΣF = 0.

Bernoulli / Pipe Flow Problems

  1. Choose two points on the same streamline where you know most variables.
  2. Identify atmospheric pressures — anywhere the fluid is open to air (top of a reservoir, exit hole, top of a fountain) P = P_atm. When both points have P_atm, the pressure terms cancel entirely.
  3. Write the full Bernoulli equation between those two points before canceling terms.
  4. Cancel what's equal — same height? ρgy terms cancel. Large reservoir (wide tank top)? v ≈ 0 there by continuity.
  5. Use continuity first if you need a speed — A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ gives the other speed.
  6. Check units — every term in Bernoulli has units of Pa (N/m²).

Must-Know Conceptual Scenarios

Hydraulic Lift

Small force on small piston → same pressure transmitted → large force on large piston. F₂ = F₁ · (A₂/A₁). Work is conserved: the small piston moves farther.

Venturi Effect

Fluid speeds up through a constriction (continuity) → pressure drops (Bernoulli). Used in carburetors, atomizers, and Venturi meters. The narrow section has lower pressure, not higher.

Torricelli's Theorem — Derivation

Apply Bernoulli from the open surface (point A, height y_A) to the exit hole (point B, y_B = 0). Both are exposed to air so P_A = P_B = P_atm — pressure cancels. The tank is large, so A_A ≫ A_B; continuity gives v_A ≈ 0. What remains: ρgy_A = ½ρv_B², giving v_B = √(2gy_A). Exit speed depends only on the height of fluid above the hole.

Apparent Weight in Fluid

W_apparent = W_actual − F_b = mg − ρ_f · V · g. This is what a scale reads when an object is submerged. A scale under the fluid reads more (reaction to buoyancy).

Submerged Object on a String

Free-body diagram: weight mg down, buoyant force F_b up, string tension T up. Equilibrium gives T = mg − F_b — tension is always less than the object's weight when submerged. If the object rests on the bottom of the container instead, a normal force F_N acts upward and T = mg − F_b − F_N, making tension even smaller (possibly zero).

Pressurized Tank Fountain

When the water source has gauge pressure P_gauge (above atmospheric), Bernoulli from source to the open exit gives v_exit = √(2P_gauge/ρ). The fountain then rises to h = v²/(2g) = P_gauge/(ρg). Fountain height is linear in source pressure, but exit speed is only √-linear — doubling the pressure raises the fountain height by a factor of 2 but only increases exit speed by √2.

Worked Examples from the Textbook

Ranking Pressures in a Non-Uniform Container

Setup: Several labeled points inside a fluid-filled container of varying width. Strategy: P = P₀ + ρgy depends only on vertical depth below the surface — container shape and horizontal position are irrelevant. Two points at the same height have equal pressure, regardless of whether the container is wide or narrow there. Takeaway: Draw a horizontal line through any point; all points on that line share the same pressure if connected by fluid.

Pipe with Halved Diameter

Setup: Fluid flows from a wide pipe into a section with half the diameter at the same height. Strategy: Area scales as d², so halving diameter quarters the area (A ∝ d²). Continuity (A₁v₁ = A₂v₂) then gives v₂ = 4v₁ — speed quadruples. Apply Bernoulli at the same height (ρgy terms cancel): P₁ + ½ρv₁² = P₂ + ½ρv₂². Since v₂ is much larger, P₂ must be lower. Takeaway: Chain continuity into Bernoulli whenever pipe geometry changes — solve for speed first, pressure second.

Gauge vs. Absolute Pressure Scaling with Density

Setup: A pressure probe sits at depth y in a container first filled with water, then with a denser fluid. Strategy: Gauge pressure = ρgy scales directly with fluid density. But absolute pressure = P_atm + ρgy changes very little near the surface because P_atm ≈ 10⁵ Pa dominates ρgy for shallow depths. Tripling fluid density triples gauge pressure but shifts absolute pressure by only a few percent. Takeaway: Gauge and absolute pressure scale very differently — know which one the problem is asking about.

5-Point Exam Strategy

Free-response approach AP FRQs award partial credit generously. Write the correct formula first, substitute values second, solve third. A correct formula with an arithmetic mistake still earns most of the points.

What earns full marks

  • State which principle you're using (Bernoulli, Archimedes, Pascal, Continuity)
  • Show the general equation before substituting
  • Justify any simplifications (e.g., "the reservoir is large so v₁ ≈ 0")
  • Include units in your final answer
  • For qualitative questions: explain the direction of change AND the reason

Fluid Properties

Density
ρ = m / V
Mass per unit volume. For water, ρ ≈ 1000 kg/m³.
ρ — density (kg/m³) m — mass (kg) V — volume (m³)
Where does this come from?

Definition: density is how much mass is packed into a unit volume. It's an intrinsic material property — cutting an object in half halves its mass and volume, so ρ stays the same. Density determines whether things float (ρ_obj < ρ_fluid) or sink (ρ_obj > ρ_fluid).

Pressure
P = F / A
Force per unit area. Pressure acts equally in all directions in a static fluid.
P — pressure (Pa = N/m²) F — force (N) A — area (m²)
Where does this come from?

Definition: pressure is force distributed over area. The same force on a smaller area creates higher pressure — why knife blades are sharp. In a static fluid, pressure is isotropic (equal in all directions) because if it weren't, fluid molecules would accelerate to equalize it. Units: 1 Pa = 1 N/m² = 1 kg/(m·s²).

Gauge Pressure
P_gauge = P − P_atm
Absolute pressure is the full pressure including atmosphere. Gauge pressure is the excess above atmospheric — what most instruments read. At the open surface, gauge pressure = 0.
P — absolute pressure (Pa) P_atm ≈ 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa P_gauge — excess above atmosphere (Pa)
Where does this come from?

Most pressure instruments measure relative to atmosphere (which surrounds us everywhere). A tire gauge reading "30 psi" means 30 psi above atmosphere. Absolute pressure = gauge + atmospheric. In fluid problems, P_atm often appears on both sides of an equation and cancels — which is why gauge pressure simplifies calculations.

Hydrostatic Pressure
P = P₀ + ρgh
Pressure at depth h below the surface. P₀ is the pressure at the surface (often atmospheric, ~101 325 Pa).
P₀ — surface pressure (Pa) ρ — fluid density (kg/m³) g — 9.8 m/s² h — depth below surface (m)
Where does this come from?

Consider a fluid column of height h, cross-section A, at rest (ΣF = 0). Weight of fluid = ρ·V·g = ρ·A·h·g. This weight pushes down on the area A below, adding pressure ΔP = weight/area = ρgh. Add the surface pressure P₀ to get total pressure: P = P₀ + ρgh. Pressure increases linearly with depth — every 10 m of water ≈ 1 additional atm.

Pascal's & Archimedes' Principles

Pascal's Principle
F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂
Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid transmits equally throughout. Basis of hydraulic systems. Work is conserved: the small piston travels farther — F₁d₁ = F₂d₂.
F₁, F₂ — input/output forces (N) A₁, A₂ — piston areas (m²)
Where does this come from?

In a static fluid, pressure transmits equally in all directions (Pascal, 1648). So the added pressure at piston 1 (= F₁/A₁) equals the pressure at piston 2 (= F₂/A₂): F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂. This is a force multiplier — larger area gets larger force. But energy is conserved: the small piston moves farther, so F₁d₁ = F₂d₂. Car brakes and hydraulic lifts exploit this.

Buoyant Force (Archimedes)
F_b = ρ_f · V_sub · g
Upward force on a submerged/floating object equals the weight of fluid displaced.
F_b — buoyant force (N) ρ_f — fluid density (kg/m³) V_sub — submerged volume (m³) g — 9.8 m/s²
Where does this come from?

Pressure increases with depth, so the upward pressure on the bottom of a submerged object is greater than the downward pressure on its top. Net upward pressure force = ΔP · A = ρ_f·g·h·A = ρ_f·g·V_sub. This equals the weight of displaced fluid: F_b = ρ_f·V_sub·g. Archimedes' principle: the buoyant force equals the weight of the fluid that would fill the submerged volume.

Floating Condition
ρ_obj / ρ_f = V_sub / V_obj
Fraction of object submerged equals the ratio of densities. Object floats when ρ_obj < ρ_f.
ρ_obj — object density (kg/m³) ρ_f — fluid density (kg/m³)
Where does this come from?

At equilibrium, F_b = W_obj: ρ_f·V_sub·g = ρ_obj·V_obj·g. Cancel g and rearrange: V_sub/V_obj = ρ_obj/ρ_f. If ρ_obj = 0.7·ρ_f, then 70% of the object is submerged. Ice in water: ρ_ice/ρ_water ≈ 0.917, so 91.7% of an iceberg is below the surface.

Fluid Flow

Volume Flow Rate
Q = A · v
Volume of fluid passing a cross-section per second. Units: m³/s.
Q — flow rate (m³/s) A — cross-sectional area (m²) v — fluid speed (m/s)
Where does this come from?

In time Δt, a fluid moving at speed v sweeps out a cylinder of length v·Δt and cross-section A. Volume = A·v·Δt. Flow rate Q = Volume/Δt = A·v. Think of it as the "pipe area times how fast fluid moves through it." Units: m² × m/s = m³/s.

Continuity Equation
A₁v₁ = A₂v₂
For an incompressible fluid in a pipe, flow rate is constant. Smaller area → faster speed.
A₁, A₂ — areas at points 1, 2 (m²) v₁, v₂ — speeds at points 1, 2 (m/s)
Where does this come from?

Conservation of mass: for an incompressible fluid (constant density), the mass entering any section per second must equal the mass leaving it. Mass flow rate = ρ·A·v = constant. Since ρ is constant, A·v = constant: A₁v₁ = A₂v₂. This is why water speeds up when you squeeze the hose nozzle — same Q, smaller A → larger v.

Bernoulli's Equation
P + ρgy + ½ρv² = const
Energy conservation per unit volume for ideal fluid flow. Higher speed → lower pressure (and vice versa). y is height above a chosen reference level.
P — pressure (Pa) ρgy — gravitational PE density (Pa) ½ρv² — kinetic energy density (Pa)
Where does this come from?

Divide the work-energy theorem by volume V for a fluid parcel. KE/V = ½ρv². PE/V = ρgh. The pressure term comes from the work done by pressure forces on the parcel: W_pressure/V = P. Combining: P + ½ρv² + ρgy = constant — this is just conservation of energy per unit volume along a streamline. Higher v means some KE came from somewhere — in a horizontal pipe, it came from pressure drop (P decreases).

Bernoulli (two points)
P₁ + ρgy₁ + ½ρv₁² = P₂ + ρgy₂ + ½ρv₂²
Apply between any two points along a streamline in steady, incompressible, non-viscous flow. y is height above a reference — pick the lower point as y = 0.
Subscripts 1, 2 — two points on streamline y₁, y₂ — heights above reference (m)
Where does this come from?

Same as above — just equate the constant at two specific points. The key assumptions are: steady flow (velocity field doesn't change in time), incompressible (ρ = constant, valid for liquids and slow gases), non-viscous (no friction between fluid layers), and along a single streamline. Violating any of these requires corrections beyond AP Physics.

Torricelli's Theorem
v = √(2gy_A)
Exit speed at a hole when the fluid surface (point A) is a height y_A above the hole. Derived from Bernoulli: both pressures are P_atm and cancel; v_A ≈ 0 by continuity (large tank).
v — exit speed at hole (m/s) y_A — height of fluid surface above hole (m)
Where does this come from?

Apply Bernoulli between the surface (point A: P = P_atm, v_A ≈ 0, h = y_A) and the hole (point B: P = P_atm, v = ?, h = 0). P_atm cancels on both sides. ρg·y_A + 0 = 0 + ½ρv² → v = √(2g·y_A). Identical to the speed of a free-falling object dropped from height y_A — a beautiful result showing Bernoulli is really just energy conservation.
